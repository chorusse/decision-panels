# General tier: the lenses every business already has

**Tier:** general. Composes underneath a sector pack and a vertical pack. See SKILL.md step 0.

**This is not a pack, and deliberately so.** It has no decision classes, no fault lines and no scout
triples. It is the small set of lenses every business owns in common, made available to any panel so
that sector and vertical packs don't each have to reinvent them.

**Why it has no fault lines:** a fault line is a tension *a particular domain* argues about, and that
is exactly what a general tier cannot know. Businesses in different fields do not split the same way.
Anything general enough to sit at this tier is too general to be a fault line, and inventing one
here would produce the confident, fieldless advice this whole method exists to avoid.

> **If a panel is leaning on this tier for the substance of a decision, the panel is thin.** That is
> a signal to say so in the step-9 verdict, not to compensate with a more assertive brief.

## The lenses

- **The Demand Lens.** *Remit:* where the next customer comes from, and whether that source is
  owned, rented or borrowed. *Argues for:* building a channel the business controls. *Bias:* treats
  marketing spend as always justified, and undercounts how well referral is already working.
  *Crux:* if the current source halved next quarter, what is the plan?
- **The Capacity Realist.** *Remit:* whose hours this actually consumes, including the owner's.
  *Argues for:* refusing work the business cannot staff. *Bias:* under-bets on growth. *Crux:* who
  does this if the owner is unavailable for two weeks?
- **The Staff Lens.** *Remit:* the people who execute it; retention, the seniority ladder, and what
  a crunch does to them. *Argues for:* decisions that keep good people past year three. *Bias:*
  privileges the team over the business's survival. *Crux:* who leaves if this goes ahead?
- **The Customer-Outcome Lens.** *Remit:* what genuinely serves the customer, including when it is
  not what they asked for. *Argues for:* the option they would choose fully informed. *Bias:* can
  privilege the customer over the business's viability. *Crux:* would you be comfortable telling
  them exactly why you chose this?

## Calibration questions worth asking in any business

- What does a good version of this business look like in five years: bigger, more profitable at the
  same size, or something you could step out of?
- How many weeks a year is the owner the binding constraint, and what breaks first when they are?
- If this goes wrong, does it cost money, a licence, or a reputation in a community you cannot
  re-enter?

## Guardrails that bind in every panel

- **Never state law, tax treatment, employment obligations or regulatory thresholds as fact.** Frame
  each as "confirm with your own adviser," and treat the answer as an input the panel does not have.
- **Never assume the business wants to grow.** Plenty of owners are optimising for income, autonomy
  or time. A panel that assumes scale is the goal is confidently wrong about the objective.
- **Never assume an exit.** Whether the owner intends to sell is a calibration question, never a
  premise, and no lens may treat "this reduces sale value" as a cost unless the user has said a
  sale is in view.
- **Never treat staff departure, customer loss or owner burnout as purely financial.** If the panel
  can only argue in dollars there, it is missing the decision.

## Seating note

Do not seat **Capacity Realist** and **Staff** as a pair without an opposing lens; they agree by
construction on nearly every workload question and will manufacture consensus.
