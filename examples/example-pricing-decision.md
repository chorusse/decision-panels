# Example run — a pricing decision

**User asks:** "We're a 6-person B2B SaaS at $2k MRR. Should we raise our price from $49/mo to $99/mo? I'm torn — we need revenue but I'm scared of churn."

This is what Decision Panels produces.

---

## Decision
> Whether to double the list price from $49 to $99/mo now, for a 6-person B2B SaaS at $2k MRR.

**Options considered:** (A) raise to $99 for all; (B) raise to $99 for *new* customers only, grandfather existing; (C) hold at $49; (D) raise to a middle point (~$79).
**Stakes / reversibility:** Medium-high. A price *increase* is hard to walk back without looking erratic; grandfathering is a two-way door.

## The recommendation
**→ Option B: raise to $99 for new customers, grandfather existing ones.** Confidence: **Medium-High**

1. At $2k MRR you have almost no data that $49 is right — a new-customer test buys pricing information at near-zero risk to current revenue.
2. Grandfathering removes the one thing that actually kills you at this size: churn from your reference customers and early advocates.
3. It's a two-way door — if $99 converts fine, roll it to everyone later from a position of evidence.

Ranking: **B over D over A over C.** D (middle) hedges the *information* you're trying to buy; A risks your base for revenue you can't yet afford to lose; C leaves you flying blind on price.

## Did the panel beat one good answer?

**Solo baseline (taken before the panel convened):** Option B · confidence **Medium** — at $2k MRR the pricing *information* is worth more than the marginal revenue, and a new-customer-only test buys it without risking the base.

**Verdict: Panel added a decisive risk.**

Same call, roughly the same confidence — but the panel surfaced the tail on grandfathering that the single answer never raised: you are committing to maintain two price tiers indefinitely, and every future increase gets *harder*, because you'll have taught your base that loyalty means immunity. Worth knowing before you commit, not after. What would have needed a panel outright: a decision where the options differ in kind rather than in degree.

**Which round did the work:** the **blind peer review**. All five independent takes converged on some form of "test on new customers"; none of them named the grandfathering tail until they read each other.

## The panel
- **The Numbers:** raise — at ~40 customers, even 30% price-sensitivity nets more revenue; the EV favors a higher price.
- **The Skeptic:** don't raise *for everyone* — you have no evidence $49 is your constraint; you might be solving the wrong problem (it may be top-of-funnel, not price).
- **The Customer Advocate:** protect the early believers — a surprise 2x on the people who took a chance on you is how you poison your first references. *(Held under pressure: reviewers argued the base is small enough to absorb; it answered that at 40 customers the references, not the revenue, are the asset.)*
- **The Runway Realist:** you need revenue, but $2k MRR means the answer is *more customers*, not squeezing 40 of them. *(Moved after review: conceded to the argument that a new-customer test costs zero current revenue and produces exactly the acquisition data it was asking for — moved from "hold" to B.)*
- **The Reversibility Lens:** a universal increase is near-irreversible in perception; a new-customer test is fully reversible.

## What the blind review caught

The panelists read each other's answers as A–E, without knowing who wrote what.

- **What everyone missed:** grandfathering is not free and not neutral — it creates a second price tier you support forever and makes the *next* increase harder, since the base learns that price rises don't apply to them. Five takes debated whether to grandfather; none costed what happens afterwards.
- **Biggest blind spot named:** the EV argument for raising treats price sensitivity as uniform across the base. At 40 customers, two or three anchor accounts likely carry the number, and losing one of those is not a 2.5% churn event — it's the quarter.
- **Who moved, and who held:** one position moved, on a named argument (above). The Customer Advocate held out and its objection was never answered — it is preserved in the dissent rather than outvoted.

## The real disagreement
**The Numbers vs. the Skeptic.** Numbers says the expected value of a higher price is clearly positive and you're leaving money on the table. The Skeptic says EV math on 40 customers is noise, and the real bottleneck is almost certainly acquisition, not price — raising price could feel like progress while the actual problem festers. Both are right about different things: the price is probably too low *and* it's probably not your binding constraint.

## The strongest case against the recommendation (preserved dissent)
If your growth problem is really top-of-funnel, a new-customer price hike *reduces* conversions right when you most need logos and learning — you'd be optimizing margin per customer while starving the customer count that actually determines whether you survive. In that world, C (hold) or even a temporary *discount* to accelerate acquisition beats any increase.

## What would change the call
Your **trial-to-paid conversion rate** and **where deals die.** If conversion is healthy and you're demand-constrained → raise with confidence (lean toward A sooner). If conversion is weak or deals stall on price → hold, and fix the funnel first. Go get that number before you touch pricing broadly.
